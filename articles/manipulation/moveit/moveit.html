MoveIt 2: Motion Planning for Robots

How Does a Robot Know How to Move?

Let's say you have a robotic arm. You know its current state, and you know where you want it to go. So how do you make it move? We have two choices either move it using joint motion or cartesian motion.At first, the problem seems simple.But real robotic motion quickly becomes more complicated.

What if we want the end-effector to reach a specific position and orientation? How do we determine a valid joint configuration for that pose? If multiple configurations are possible, how do we choose one? What if the robot has joint limits or self-collision constraints? What if there are obstacles in the workspace and the robot cannot simply move directly to the target? How do we find a collision-free path through the environment and turn it into a trajectory that the robot controller can execute?

These are motion-planning problems. This is where MoveIt2 comes into the picture.

What Is MoveIt 2?

MoveIt 2 is a motion-planning framework built for ROS 2. It provides tools and APIs for planning and executing robot motion while considering the robot's kinematics, joint limits, collisions, constraints, and the desired target.

Instead of manually determining every intermediate joint position required to move a robot from one configuration to another, we can specify a goal and allow MoveIt 2 to determine a suitable trajectory.

The important point is that MoveIt 2 primarily answers:

"How can the robot move from its current state to the desired state?"

It is not itself the low-level motor controller.

Where Does MoveIt 2 Sit in the Robot Control Stack?

To understand the role of MoveIt 2, it is important to distinguish motion planning from motion control. MoveIt 2 does not directly control the motors of a robot. Instead, it operates at a higher level, generating a trajectory that can be executed by the robot's controllers.

Robot Application

The application defines the task the robot needs to perform, such as moving to a target, assembling a component, or performing a pick-and-place operation.

↓
MoveIt 2 — Motion Planning

MoveIt 2 receives the robot state, target, and environment and generates a feasible motion trajectory while considering constraints such as joint limits and collisions.

↓
ros2_control — Robot Controllers

The generated trajectory is passed to the appropriate controller, which is responsible for making the robot follow the commanded motion.

↓
Hardware Interface

The hardware interface connects the ROS 2 control system to the actual robot hardware or a simulation.

↓
Robot

The robot executes the commanded motion through its actuators and low-level control system.

Therefore, MoveIt 2 should be viewed as the motion-planning layer rather than the low-level control layer.

Why Do We Need Motion Planning?

Suppose we know the robot's current configuration and have a desired target. A simple approach would be to calculate the required joint positions and command the robot to move there. But reaching a target is only part of the problem.

A practical robot must move through a physical environment while respecting the limitations of its own mechanical structure. This raises several questions:

  • Can the robot reach the desired target?
  • Are the required joint configurations within the robot's joint limits?
  • Could the robot collide with itself while moving?
  • Could it collide with an object in the environment?
  • If several valid configurations exist, which one should be used?
  • What path should the robot follow between the current state and the target?

Solving the kinematics of a robot can determine whether a particular pose is reachable, but motion planning considers the larger problem of finding a valid path through the robot's configuration space while respecting the constraints of the robot and its environment.

This is the problem that motion-planning frameworks such as MoveIt 2 are designed to address.

What Does MoveIt 2 Provide?

MoveIt 2 brings together several components required to plan robot motion. Instead of implementing each part of the planning pipeline independently, a robot application can use the interfaces provided by MoveIt 2.

Motion Planning

MoveIt 2 can generate a feasible trajectory between the robot's current state and a desired goal while considering the constraints provided to the planner.

Inverse Kinematics

When a target is specified as an end-effector pose, MoveIt 2 can use an inverse-kinematics solver to determine joint configurations that can achieve the requested pose.

Collision Checking

MoveIt 2 can evaluate whether robot configurations and planned motions result in collisions between the robot's own links or with objects represented in the planning scene.

Constraints

Motion planning can be performed subject to constraints such as joint limits, position constraints, and orientation constraints.

Planning Scene

MoveIt 2 maintains a representation of the robot and its environment through the planning scene. Objects can be added to this environment so that the planner can consider them while generating a trajectory.

These capabilities work together to transform a high-level motion goal into a trajectory that can be passed to the robot's controller.

Types of Motion in MoveIt 2

MoveIt 2 provides several ways to describe how we want a robot to move. The appropriate method depends on whether the goal is defined in terms of a predefined configuration, joint values, an end-effector pose, or a specific Cartesian path.

1. Named Target

A named target represents a predefined robot configuration. Instead of specifying individual joint values, we can give the configuration a name such as home or ready and ask MoveIt 2 to move the robot to that configuration.

2. Joint-Space Target

A joint-space target directly specifies the desired value of each joint. MoveIt 2 then plans a trajectory from the current joint configuration to the requested configuration while considering the configured constraints.

3. Pose Target

A pose target specifies the desired position and orientation of the robot's end-effector. MoveIt 2 can use inverse kinematics to determine a suitable joint configuration and then plan a motion to reach it.

4. Cartesian Path

A Cartesian path specifies how the end-effector should move through Cartesian space. Instead of only specifying the final pose, we can define waypoints that the end-effector should follow, allowing applications to generate motions such as straight-line movements.

These four approaches differ mainly in how the motion goal is expressed. MoveIt 2 can then use its planning and trajectory generation capabilities to produce a motion that the robot controller can execute.

Motion Planning vs Motion Control

MoveIt 2 is responsible for planning robot motion, but planning a motion is different from controlling the robot to execute that motion.

Motion planning determines a feasible path and trajectory from the current robot state to a desired goal. It considers the robot's configuration, constraints, and environment to determine how the robot should move.

Motion control is responsible for making the physical robot follow that trajectory. Controllers operate at a lower level and generate the commands required by the robot's actuators.

This separation is important because MoveIt 2 does not replace the controller. Instead, it works together with the control layer to turn a planned motion into actual robot movement.

Collision Checking and the Planning Scene

Reaching a target is not enough for a robot. The robot must also be able to reach that target without colliding with itself or with objects in its environment.

MoveIt 2 uses a Planning Scene to represent the robot and relevant objects around it. This allows the motion planner to evaluate whether a planned configuration or trajectory is valid.

For example, consider a robot that needs to move its end-effector from one side of an obstacle to the other:

A simple motion directly toward the target may pass through the obstacle. A motion planner can instead search for an alternative collision-free path.

Collision checking can also consider self-collisions, where different links of the robot could come into contact with each other.

This is one of the important distinctions between solving only the robot's kinematics and solving a broader motion-planning problem: the robot must not only be able to reach the target, but must also find a valid way to get there.

The MoveIt 2 Planning Pipeline

When a motion request is sent to MoveIt 2, several components work together to transform the request into an executable trajectory. 

Motion Request
      ↓
Planning Scene
      ↓
Motion Planner
      ↓
Collision Checking
      ↓
Trajectory Processing
      ↓
Robot Controller

The planning scene provides the planner with the current robot state and information about relevant objects in the environment.

The motion planner searches for a valid path between the current state and the requested goal. Different planning algorithms can be used depending on the requirements of the application.

During planning, candidate motions can be checked for collisions and other constraints. Once a valid solution is found, the resulting path can be processed into a trajectory suitable for execution.

Finally, the trajectory is passed to the appropriate robot controller, which executes the planned motion.

The important idea is that MoveIt 2 is not a single algorithm. It is a collection of components that work together to turn a high-level motion request into a feasible robot trajectory.

Limitations of MoveIt 2

MoveIt 2 provides a powerful motion-planning framework, but it is not a complete solution for every aspect of robotic manipulation. It is important to understand what MoveIt 2 does and what still needs to be implemented by the application.

Motion Planning Is Not Task Planning

MoveIt 2 can plan how the robot should move between states, but it does not automatically decide the complete sequence of actions required for a task. For example, a pick-and-place application still needs higher-level logic to decide which object to pick, where to place it, and when to open or close the gripper.

It Does Not Provide Perception

MoveIt 2 can use objects represented in its planning scene, but it does not automatically detect objects in the real world. Cameras, sensors, and perception algorithms are still required to determine where objects are located.

Planning Is Not Low-Level Control

MoveIt 2 generates motion plans and trajectories, but the robot still requires controllers to execute those trajectories. Applications that require low-level position, velocity, impedance, or torque control need an appropriate control framework in addition to MoveIt 2.

No Guarantee of the Globally Optimal Path

A motion planner can find a valid solution without necessarily finding the globally optimal one. The quality of the resulting motion depends on the selected planner, planning parameters, constraints, and available planning time.

Therefore, MoveIt 2 should be viewed as one component of a larger robotic system. It is particularly useful for motion planning and collision-aware trajectory generation, while perception, task-level reasoning, and low-level control remain separate concerns.

Conclusion

MoveIt 2 provides a structured way to solve the motion-planning problem in ROS 2. Instead of manually handling every aspect of finding a feasible robot trajectory, applications can define a motion goal and rely on MoveIt 2 to plan a suitable path while considering the robot model, constraints, and environment.

Its capabilities range from simple joint and named-target motions to end-effector pose goals and Cartesian paths, with collision checking and trajectory generation forming an important part of the planning process.

At the same time, MoveIt 2 is only one component of a complete robotic system. Perception, task-level decision making, grasping logic, and low-level control are separate responsibilities that must work together with the motion-planning layer.

MoveIt 2 can also serve as the motion-execution layer for higher-level planners. A task planner, behavior planner, or autonomous decision-making system can determine what the robot should accomplish and provide MoveIt 2 with the corresponding motion goals, such as a target pose, joint configuration, or Cartesian path. MoveIt 2 can then take those goals and handle the lower-level motion-planning problem of finding a feasible trajectory.

Understanding this separation is essential when building robotic applications with ROS 2. MoveIt 2 handles the complexity of planning how the robot should move, while the rest of the system determines what the robot should do and how the physical hardware should execute that motion.
